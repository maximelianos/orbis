\title{What does a driving world model know about the future?\\
\large Probing Orbis with a flow-matching trajectory model on NAVSIM}
\author{\students}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
    \maketitle

    \begin{abstract}
    We ask how much of the future ego trajectory is stored in the frozen
    features of Orbis, a video world model for driving trained on raw front-camera
    video. A small flow-matching probe reads the features of the last four camera
    frames and samples trajectories for the next four seconds, in the NAVSIM
    protocol. Because the probe is generative, we score the whole predicted
    distribution with the energy score and a dispersion ratio, not only its mean.
    Our first question is why the sampled distribution became narrower with
    training in our earlier experiments. We find that part of the narrowing came
    from integrating the sampler with too few steps, and the rest is
    memorization: it appears only when the probe can fit the training set (high
    capacity or little data), and the training step at which it starts grows
    linearly with the number of training scenes, as recent theory of diffusion
    models predicts. A polynomial trajectory code makes the targets easier to
    learn but does not prevent the collapse. Our second question is which
    representation holds the most driving information. Under one common feature
    interface, the middle block of the Orbis world model beats DINOv2, V-JEPA~2
    and the Orbis tokenizer latents, both for the probe and for linear read-out of
    ego speed and acceleration. Finally, we test whether the features are useful
    for planning. The diffusion probe reaches an EPDMS of 0.81 on navtest, and a
    one-pass planner on the same frozen features reaches 0.88 after two minutes
    of training, above the privileged PDM-Closed planner. The probes do not
    generalize to unseen combinations of city and driving command, and they
    remain overconfident when they fail.
    \end{abstract}
