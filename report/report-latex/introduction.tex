\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A driving world model learns to predict future camera frames from past ones. To
do this well it has to track how the scene moves and how the ego vehicle moves
through it. So its internal features may already contain much of what a planner
needs, without any driving labels. This report tests that idea on
Orbis~\cite{orbis}, a flow-matching video world model trained only on raw
front-camera video.

We freeze Orbis and train a small probe on its features to predict the future
ego trajectory. If a small probe succeeds, the information was in the features
and not learned by the probe. The probe is generative: a flow-matching model
that samples trajectories, conditioned on the features. We chose a generative
probe because driving is multimodal. At a junction the same past can continue
left, straight or right, and a regression head trained with an $L_2$ loss
predicts the average of these, a path no driver takes. A generative probe can
represent all of them, and its samples tell us what the features say about the
uncertainty of the future, not only about its mean.

Our earlier experiments with this probe raised a problem. The sampled
trajectories started as a wide fan and became one narrow bundle as training went
on, sometimes on the wrong side of a junction. We wanted to know whether this is
a property of the features, of the probe, or of how we measured it. And we
wanted to compare Orbis with other frozen encoders under a fair setup, which the
earlier pipeline did not allow. It also did not follow the NAVSIM
protocol~\cite{navsim1}, so its numbers could not be compared with published
planners. We therefore rebuilt the experiments around the NAVSIM protocol
(Section~\ref{sec:data}) and asked three questions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Why does the predicted distribution collapse during training, and what
  prevents it? (Section~\ref{sec:collapse})
  \item Which frozen representation holds the most information about the future
  trajectory, and where in the world model is it? (Section~\ref{sec:features})
  \item Are these features useful for planning when scored with the official
  NAVSIM metric? (Section~\ref{sec:planning})
\end{enumerate}

The main findings are these.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item The collapse is memorization of the training set. It appears when the
  probe has enough capacity or too little data, and its onset moves later in
  proportion to the number of training scenes, as predicted for diffusion models
  by Bonnaire et al.~\cite{bonnaire}. A part of the earlier narrowing was not
  collapse at all but an artefact of sampling with too few ODE steps.
  \item The middle of the Orbis world model (block 11 of 24, at the last observed
  frame) is the best of all tested representations. It lowers the energy score by
  37\% against V-JEPA~2 and 46\% against DINOv2, and a linear map reads the ego
  speed from it with $R^2=0.95$.
  \item On the frozen features, a one-pass planner trained for two minutes reaches
  EPDMS 0.881 on navtest. The diffusion probe reaches 0.810, and the best of four
  of its samples 0.900, so the samples are good but picking one is hard.
  \item The probes fail on unseen combinations of city and driving command, and
  they stay as confident as on familiar inputs when they fail.
\end{itemize}
