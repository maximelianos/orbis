\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The project sits at the intersection of four lines of research:
\emph{generative modelling} through diffusion and flow matching, the
\emph{diffusion transformer} architecture that has become the workhorse of
modern generative models, \emph{world models} for autonomous driving and
navigation, and the \emph{trajectory planners} that produce the same kind of
output as our probe. Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} organizes the generative and
world-model works along the first three axes. We then treat each line in turn:
Section~\ref{sec:diffusion-theory} develops the theory of diffusion and flow
matching, Section~\ref{sec:dit} describes the diffusion transformer,
Section~\ref{sec:world-models} reviews world models from the general concept
through to the driving and navigation models that motivate our probe, and
Section~\ref{sec:planners} goes through the planning literature, from classical
rule-based methods to learned end-to-end and world-model-based ones, and says
where the probe sits among them.

\subsection{Diffusion and flow matching}
\label{sec:diffusion-theory}

\paragraph{Denoising diffusion probabilistic models.}
Diffusion models~\cite{ho_ddpm} define a generative process by reversing a
fixed forward process that gradually corrupts data into noise. Given a data
sample $\mathbf{x}_0$, the forward process produces a sequence of increasingly
noisy variables $\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T$ according to a Gaussian
transition with a predefined variance schedule $\{\alpha_t\}$. A convenient
property is that any noise level can be sampled in closed form,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0
+ \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\qquad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\alpha}_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$. The model learns the reverse
process $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$, which gradually denoises a
pure-noise sample back to data. Training reduces to a simple regression in
which a network $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ predicts the noise that was added,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
\big[\,\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)
- \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \rVert_2^2\,\big].
\end{equation}
New samples come from initializing $\mathbf{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and
iterating the learned reverse transitions. For conditional generation the
standard mechanism is \emph{classifier-free guidance}~\cite{ho_ddpm}. The model
is trained both with and without the conditioning $c$, the latter by dropping $c$
for a learned null embedding, and at sampling time the two predictions are
extrapolated,
$\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}(\mathbf{x}_t,c)
=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,\varnothing)
+ s\big(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,c)
-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,\varnothing)\big)$,
with guidance scale $s>1$ trading diversity for fidelity to the condition.

\paragraph{Flow matching.}
Flow matching~\cite{lipman_fm} reframes generation as learning a continuous
\emph{velocity field} that transports a simple prior distribution to the data
distribution along a probability path, rather than reversing a discrete
noising chain. The simplest and most widely used instance defines a linear
(``rectified'') path between a data point $\mathbf{x}$ and a noise sample
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}^{\tau} = (1-\tau)\,\mathbf{x} + \tau\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\qquad \tau\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
whose time-derivative is the constant target velocity
$\tfrac{d\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}{d\tau}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}$. The model
$\mathbf{v}_\theta$ is trained to regress this velocity,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{\tau,\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
\big[\,\lVert \mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}^{\tau},\tau)
- (\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\mathbf{x}) \rVert_2^2\,\big],
\label{eq:fm-loss}
\end{equation}
and samples come from integrating the ordinary differential equation
$\tfrac{d\mathbf{x}^{\tau}}{d\tau}=\mathbf{v}_\theta(\mathbf{x}^{\tau},\tau)$
from the prior at $\tau=1$ back to the data manifold at $\tau=0$. Flow matching
can be read as a continuous-time generalization of diffusion. The denoising
intuition is the same, but the transport paths are straighter, so fewer
integration steps are needed and training tends to be more stable. This is the
objective Orbis uses for its continuous world model
(Equations~\eqref{eq:fm-forward-intro} and~\eqref{eq:fm-loss-intro}), and it
suits the trajectory head of our probe as well, since the 2D trajectory space is
low-dimensional and benefits from the short sampling schedules flow matching
allows.

\paragraph{Continuous vs.\ discrete generation.}
One design axis keeps coming back, and Orbis makes it explicit: whether to model
a continuous latent, with diffusion or flow matching, or a discrete sequence of
codebook tokens, with autoregressive or masked generative
modelling~\cite{maskgit}. Discrete token models inherit the machinery of large
language models and look attractive for long rollouts. On driving video, though,
Orbis finds the continuous flow-matching formulation less brittle to design
choices and much better over long horizons. Both families need a (VQ-)VAE-style
tokenizer~\cite{vqgan} to map pixels to a compact latent, and the hybrid Orbis
tokenizer supports both at once.

\subsection{The diffusion transformer}
\label{sec:dit}

Early diffusion models used convolutional U-Net backbones. The Diffusion
Transformer (DiT)~\cite{dit} showed that the U-Net inductive bias is not
essential. A standard transformer working on latent patches matches or beats
U-Nets, and it \emph{scales} predictably: sample quality (FID) improves smoothly
as the model's compute in Gflops goes up, whether that comes from depth, width or
token count. DiT works in the latent space of a pretrained VAE. The spatial
latent is ``patchified'' into a sequence of tokens, run through a stack of
transformer blocks, and decoded back into a noise and covariance prediction. The
main design question is how to inject the conditioning, which is the diffusion
timestep $t$ and any class or context signal $c$. Of the three candidates,
in-context tokens, cross-attention and adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN), the
\emph{adaLN-Zero} variant works best. The conditioning vector regresses per-block
scale and shift parameters $(\gamma,\beta)$ for the layer norms plus an extra
residual scale $\alpha$, zero-initialized so each block starts out as the
identity. This is the mechanism Orbis uses to inject the diffusion time and the
optional ego-motion signal, and it is also the cheapest of the three.

Figure~\ref{fig:dit} sketches the block. A token sequence comes in, AdaLN
modulates a multi-head self-attention sub-layer and then a feed-forward sub-layer,
and residual connections scaled by $\alpha$ wrap both. Stack $N$ of these blocks,
condition every one on $(t,c)$, and that is the backbone. Driving and navigation
world models extend the design along the \emph{temporal} axis. Orbis interleaves
spatial and temporal attention inside each block (an STDiT), so a token attends
both to other patches in the same frame and to the same region across frames. The
navigation world model NWM~\cite{nwm} takes a different route with a Conditional
Diffusion Transformer (CDiT), which keeps the expensive self-attention on the
target frame only and reads the context frames by cross-attention. That makes the
cost \emph{linear} in the number of context frames instead of quadratic, which is
what lets it scale to a billion parameters. The scheme in Figure~\ref{fig:dit} is
the common ancestor of all of these.

\caption{The diffusion transformer (DiT) block. Patchified latent tokens pass

\subsection{World models}
\label{sec:world-models}

\paragraph{The world-model concept.}
A world model is a learned model of an environment's dynamics. Given the current
state, or a history of observations, and optionally an action, it predicts the
next state and possibly a reward. The point is that an agent with such a model
can ``imagine'' the outcome of candidate actions internally, which helps with
sample-efficient reinforcement learning, with representation learning and with
planning. World models were originally studied in games and other simulated
environments, where the dynamics are clean and interaction is unlimited. What
changed is the recent wave of high-quality video generative models, which made it
practical to learn world models straight from raw video of the real world, so the
predictive model becomes a learned simulator. For driving this is especially
appealing, because raw front-camera video is abundant and unlabeled, so a video
world model can avoid a lot of the manual perception engineering a conventional
stack needs.

\paragraph{Popular video and driving world models.}
Several lines of work have made video prediction a workable substrate for world
modelling. In games and simulation, DIAMOND and GameNGen use diffusion models to
emulate engines such as Atari and Doom from frames and actions. For driving,
GAIA-1 was an influential early demonstration of controllable future generation
conditioned on action and text, built on a discrete token model. Diffusion-based
driving models such as Vista and GEM fine-tune general-purpose pretrained video
diffusion backbones, Stable Video Diffusion among them, to produce
high-resolution, high-frame-rate rollouts of up to about fifteen seconds, and
DrivingWorld pushes rollout coherence further. Orbis argues against this trend.
Its observation is that these large pretrained-backbone models degrade quickly
over long horizons and on demanding maneuvers such as turns, where they often
stop early or drift into paths no car would take. To put a number on that, Orbis
introduces a distribution-level trajectory-based evaluation: generated videos are
mapped to pose sequences, and realism and coverage are measured with
precision/recall, Fréchet distance and average displacement error. The reason is
that standard video metrics such as FVD are insensitive to scene dynamics and
ego-motion. The same reasoning applies to our probe, which also has to be judged
on the \emph{distribution} of trajectories rather than on a single point
estimate.

\paragraph{Orbis.}
Orbis~\cite{orbis}, described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:orbis}, contributes
two things that matter here. It is trained from raw video only and is far more
economical than its competitors, $469$M parameters and $280$ hours of video
against the thousands of hours and larger backbones used elsewhere, which
suggests the learned latent is an efficient general representation rather than a
memorized one. And its hybrid tokenizer allows a controlled comparison between
continuous and discrete world models, which comes out in favour of the continuous
flow-matching version. The consequence for this report is that Orbis gives us a
compact, well-trained, raw-video latent, and how informative that latent is for
downstream driving tasks is an open question. That is what the attentive
diffusion probe is built to measure.

\paragraph{Navigation world models.}
The Navigation World Model (NWM)~\cite{nwm} is the closest precedent to our
study, because it makes the planning use of a video world model explicit. NWM is
a controllable video generation model trained on egocentric video of human and
robotic agents together with their navigation actions
$(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta\phi)$ and a time shift. It uses the CDiT architecture
from Section~\ref{sec:dit} to autoregressively predict future latent states from
past states and actions. The interesting part is what happens after training: NWM
is used for \emph{planning}. It simulates candidate action sequences and scores
how well the imagined future reaches a goal, either optimizing actions from
scratch in a model-predictive-control loop or ranking trajectories proposed by an
external policy. That is evidence for the broader claim behind our work, namely
that the representation and rollouts of a video world model carry usable
information about future motion. NWM \emph{generates and scores} full future
videos in order to plan. Our probe takes the cheaper route and reads the world
model's latent directly, training a small diffusion head to predict the future 2D
trajectory, which tests how much of that planning-relevant information is already
accessible in the frozen Orbis latent without much further processing.

\subsection{Trajectory planners}
\label{sec:planners}

What the probe outputs, a future ego trajectory in the vehicle frame predicted
from camera images, is the same object a driving planner outputs. It is
therefore useful to look at how planners are built: partly to have a sense of
what a reasonable trajectory predictor scores on the benchmarks we use, and
partly to be clear about what the probe is not.

The usual split in this literature is ``modular'' versus ``end-to-end'', but
that distinction explains rather little, and both surveys we rely on
here~\cite{e2esurvey,e2eplansurvey} argue that other questions separate the
methods better. We use three of them:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item What sits between the sensors and the trajectory? A hand-built pipeline
(perception, tracking, prediction, planning) with an explicit cost function; a
learned pipeline that keeps those modules but makes them differentiable and
trains them for the planning loss; or a single network mapping sensors to a
trajectory.
\item How is the trajectory produced? By search or optimization over a dynamics
model, by regressing waypoints, by picking from a fixed set of candidates, by
sampling from a generative model, or by generating many candidates and learning
to rank them.
\item What trains it? Hand-written rules, human demonstrations, distillation
from a privileged teacher, reward from a simulator, or an auxiliary prediction
loss.
\end{enumerate}

Table~\ref{tab:planner-classes} classifies a set of representative systems along
all three.

\paragraph{Rule-based and optimization planners.}
Before learned planners took over, the algorithms fell into four groups. Graph
search discretizes the configuration space and runs Dijkstra, A$^*$ or hybrid
A$^*$ over it, either on a cell grid or on a lattice of kinematically feasible
motion primitives attached to the lane graph. Sampling-based planners (RRT, the
asymptotically optimal RRT$^*$ and BIT$^*$, PRM) grow a tree or roadmap directly
in continuous space. Interpolating-curve methods fit polynomials or splines in
the Frenet frame of a reference centerline, which decouples lateral from
longitudinal motion and keeps the search low-dimensional. The fourth group
solves a receding-horizon optimization problem with explicit collision and
comfort constraints.

All four assume the same thing: that the world state is available explicitly, as
an HD map together with tracked objects and forecast agent trajectories, and
that the objective can be written down as a cost. The output is then
interpretable and kinematically feasible by construction. What it costs is a
full perception stack upstream, plus a cost function that has to be tuned by
hand for each scenario type.

The version that matters for benchmarking is PDM-Closed~\cite{pdm}, which wraps
the Intelligent Driver Model~\cite{idm}, a car-following rule from 2000, in a
model-predictive-control loop. At each step it produces $15$ proposals: five
target speeds between $20\%$ and $100\%$ of the speed limit, crossed with three
lateral offsets from the centerline ($-1$, $0$, $+1$\,m). Each proposal is
simulated forward for $4$\,s with a kinematic bicycle model under IDM
longitudinal control and scored with the nuPlan closed-loop metric, and the best
one is executed. It won the 2023 nuPlan challenge, and in
Table~\ref{tab:planner-numbers} it beats every learned entry in closed loop by
$20$ to $40$ points. That is the reference point we keep coming back to.

\paragraph{Imitation learning and where it breaks.}
Learned policies start with ALVINN~\cite{alvinn} and PilotNet~\cite{pilotnet},
which regress steering from a front camera, and with conditional imitation
learning~\cite{cil}, which adds a navigation command so the network knows which
way to go at an intersection. Two problems have driven most of the work since.

The first is covariate shift. Behaviour cloning is fitted on the states the
expert visited, but at test time the policy visits the states its own mistakes
produce, and the errors accumulate. The second is causal
confusion~\cite{causalconfusion}: nothing stops a discriminative model from
keying on something that merely correlates with the expert action instead of
something that causes it. The standard example is the car's own past velocity,
which produces the ``inertia problem'', where a stopped vehicle never pulls away
again. Counter-intuitively, giving the network more input can make this worse.

The usual fixes are perturbing the expert trajectory and adding synthesized
recovery examples together with auxiliary losses
(ChauffeurNet~\cite{chauffeurnet}); removing ego history from the input;
collecting data online in the style of DAgger; distilling from a privileged
teacher that sees ground-truth state (Learning by Cheating~\cite{lbc}); and
fine-tuning with policy gradients through a differentiable simulator (Urban
Driver~\cite{urbandriver}).

\paragraph{From sensor fusion to planning-oriented networks.}
TransFuser~\cite{transfuser} is the baseline most of the field compares against.
It fuses multi-view camera and LiDAR features into a BEV representation with
transformer blocks and decodes waypoints with a GRU, and NAVSIM ships it as its
reference agent. TCP~\cite{tcp} showed that predicting a trajectory and
predicting controls are complementary and worth combining.

UniAD~\cite{uniad} made the argument that gave this line of work its name.
Detection, tracking, mapping, motion forecasting and occupancy are all kept, but
as query-based differentiable modules that feed the planner and take gradients
from it, so the intermediate representations end up shaped by the planning
objective rather than by their own metrics. VAD~\cite{vad} replaced the dense
rasterized BEV with vectorized map and agent tokens, which is several times
cheaper, and VADv2~\cite{vadv2} swapped trajectory regression for a distribution
over a precomputed anchor vocabulary. PARA-Drive~\cite{paradrive} then observed
that the modules need not run in sequence at all, and got close to a $3\times$
speedup by running them in parallel at comparable accuracy.

\paragraph{Four ways of producing the trajectory.}
This is the axis our probe takes a position on, so it is worth going through in
some detail.

Regression is the simple option: one head, waypoints, an $L_1$ or $L_2$ loss. It
does well on displacement metrics. The problem is that the minimizer of a squared
loss is the conditional mean, so where the data is genuinely multi-modal (going
straight and turning left are both plausible) the network learns the average of
the two. That average can be a path that is neither, and possibly not even
drivable.

Classification avoids this by fixing $K$ candidate trajectories in advance,
usually k-means centroids over the nuPlan logs with $K$ in the thousands, and
predicting a categorical distribution over them. VADv2~\cite{vadv2} and
Hydra-MDP~\cite{hydra_mdp} work this way. The output is multi-modal by
construction and easy to rank, but the resolution is capped by whatever the
vocabulary contains.

Generative heads model the trajectory distribution directly.
Diffusion-Planner~\cite{diffusionplanner} denoises ego and neighbour
trajectories jointly with a DiT backbone and is state of the art on nuPlan.
DiffusionDrive~\cite{diffusiondrive} starts the reverse process from anchored
Gaussians rather than from pure noise, which lets it cut the number of denoising
steps from $20$ to $2$; it reports $88.1$ PDMS on NAVSIM at $45$\,FPS. This is
the family the probe belongs to, for the reason given two paragraphs above.

Propose-and-score generates many candidates, learns a scorer, and takes the
arg\,max. Hydra-MDP~\cite{hydra_mdp} distills an ensemble of rule-based metric
teachers, one head per sub-score of the PDM score, alongside the human teacher,
so that the scoring happens inside the network instead of in non-differentiable
post-processing; it won the 2024 NAVSIM challenge. Hydra-MDP++~\cite{hydra}
extends the set of experts and is where the EPDMS formulation of
Section~\ref{sec:pdms} comes from. GTRS~\cite{gtrs} combines a diffusion
proposal generator with a scorer trained under dropout on a much denser
vocabulary than it sees at inference, and won the 2025 NAVSIM v2 challenge.
DriveSuprim~\cite{drivesuprim} adds coarse-to-fine filtering of the candidate
set, rotation augmentation and self-distillation.

There is a pattern here. The strongest end-to-end systems have converged on the
same propose-and-score structure as PDM-Closed, with the hand-written simulator
replaced by a learned scorer that was trained to imitate it.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning.}
RL trains on the states the policy itself visits, so covariate shift is not an
issue, but it needs a simulator that reacts and a reward function.
Think2Drive~\cite{think2drive} learns a DreamerV3-style latent model of CARLA and
plans inside it. CaRL~\cite{carl} argues that the bottleneck is reward
engineering rather than the algorithm, and shows that a much simpler reward
scaled to $300$M samples, $30\times$ more than earlier work, does better; it
reports $64$ Driving Score on CARLA \texttt{longest6}~v2. In practice this line
of work stays inside simulation, because computing the reward needs an
environment that responds to the agent.

\paragraph{VLM-based planners.}
A newer group puts a vision-language model in the loop. EMMA~\cite{emma}
fine-tunes Gemini to emit the planned trajectory as text, together with
detections and the road graph. DriveVLM~\cite{drivevlm} runs a slow VLM that
proposes a coarse trajectory and a fast end-to-end model that refines it.
Senna~\cite{senna} restricts the VLM output to a discrete meta-action, which then
conditions VAD. The motivation is semantic context, plus an interpretability
story that comes almost for free. Whether the language actually improves the
trajectory or only the explanation attached to it is still
open~\cite{e2eplansurvey}.

\paragraph{World models used for planning.}
This is where our work sits, and the term covers three fairly different things.

The first is representation pre-training: the encoder is trained with a
self-supervised predictive objective on unlabeled video, and the features are
then used for planning. PPGeo~\cite{ppgeo} learns pose and depth from
uncalibrated YouTube driving footage and then predicts ego-motion;
ViDAR~\cite{vidar} pretrains by forecasting future point clouds.

The second is imagination and scoring. Drive-WM~\cite{drivewm} generates
multi-view future video for each candidate maneuver and picks one using an
image-based reward. WoTE~\cite{wote} does the rollout in a BEV latent instead of
in pixels, which is cheap enough to score candidates online. NWM~\cite{nwm},
covered in Section~\ref{sec:world-models}, optimizes actions in an MPC loop over
generated video.

The third keeps a conventional planner and adds a latent prediction loss on the
side. LAW~\cite{law} predicts future visual latents from the current features and
the planned trajectory. World4Drive~\cite{world4drive} builds an intention-aware
latent world model and drops perception annotations altogether.

One caveat from the survey~\cite{e2eplansurvey} is directly relevant to us: good
video prediction does not by itself mean the model is choosing safe
trajectories. That is an argument for measuring what the latent contains rather
than assuming it.

\paragraph{Why open-loop numbers are not enough.}
Two results are worth keeping in mind when reading any open-loop score. The
first is that on nuPlan the open-loop and closed-loop numbers are
anti-correlated, not merely uncorrelated. In
Table~\ref{tab:planner-numbers}, PDM-Open is top on the open-loop score ($86$
OLS) and near the bottom in closed loop ($50$ CLS-NR), while PDM-Closed is the
other way round~\cite{pdm}. The reasons are structural: open-loop evaluation
compares against the expert's own states, it punishes alternative maneuvers that
would have been fine, and there are no agent reactions in
it~\cite{e2eplansurvey}.

The second is that open-loop planning on nuScenes is close to trivial. An MLP
that sees only the ego status, with no scene input at all, is
competitive~\cite{admlp,egostatus}, because the last few seconds of ego motion
almost determine the next three. NAVSIM's pseudo-simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:datasets}) is the response to this. It keeps the cheap,
non-sequential evaluation but replaces displacement error with scores derived
from simulation. It does not fix everything: the winning entry of the 2025
NAVSIM v2 challenge reached roughly $53$ EPDMS on the \texttt{navhard} split,
against over $90$ PDMS on \texttt{navtest}.

\paragraph{Where the probe sits.}
Next to everything above, our setup is stripped down on purpose. There is no HD
map, no tracked agents, no agent forecasts, no scorer and no closed loop, only a
single frozen Orbis latent and a small flow-matching head. It belongs to the
representation branch of the world-model family (right-hand column of
Figure~\ref{fig:planner-taxonomy}), but it is stricter than PPGeo, ViDAR or LAW,
since we neither fine-tune the encoder nor train the predictive loss alongside.
The latent is fixed, which is what lets a positive result say something about the
representation rather than about the training recipe.

Three points from this section carry over into the experiments. The head has to
be generative and not a regressor, because a squared loss on multi-modal data
gives an averaged path. The baselines to compare against are the ones that bound
the task from below, constant velocity and the ego-status MLP, plus TransFuser
as a trained reference; the propose-and-score systems at the top of the
leaderboard consume far more supervision than a probe does, so they are not a
fair comparison. And displacement error on its own does not show much, which is
why we also use PDMS/EPDMS and the distribution-level view taken from Orbis.
