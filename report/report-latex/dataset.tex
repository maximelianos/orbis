\section{Datasets and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:datasets}

To evaluate predicted future trajectories we need two things: real driving data
with ground-truth ego motion, and a sensible way of scoring a predicted
trajectory. This section covers both. \textbf{nuPlan} supplies the driving logs
and defines the closed-loop planning task, and \textbf{NAVSIM} supplies a
reproducible scoring protocol built on the Predictive Driver Model Score. Orbis
is trained and evaluated partly on nuPlan, so staying with the same data and the
same metrics keeps our results comparable to it.

\subsection{nuPlan}
\label{sec:nuplan}

nuPlan~\cite{nuplan} was the first large-scale closed-loop planning benchmark
for autonomous driving built around machine learning. It contains roughly
$1500$ hours of human driving from four cities: Boston, Pittsburgh, Las Vegas
and Singapore. The cities were chosen because they drive differently. Las Vegas
has dense casino pick-up and drop-off zones and eight-lane intersections, Boston
has double-parking, Pittsburgh has its own conventions about left-turn
precedence, and Singapore drives on the left. Each city comes with a semantic
map and an efficient query API for it, and the logs contain lidar point clouds,
camera images, localization and steering inputs. The trajectories of other
agents are auto-labeled by an offline perception system (PointPillars with
CenterPoint detection and non-causal tracking) to close to human accuracy, and
intervals are tagged with scenario types such as merges, lane changes, protected
and unprotected turns, interactions with pedestrians and cyclists, or
double-parked vehicles. That tagging is what makes scenario-specific evaluation
possible.

The idea nuPlan is built on is that \emph{prediction} and \emph{planning} are
not the same problem, and that the difference shows up in how you evaluate.
Prediction benchmarks ask for the likely future trajectories of \emph{other}
agents and score them with displacement metrics such as minADE, minFDE and miss
rate. Those metrics do not carry over to ego planning. One reason is that
planning is multi-modal at the level of decisions: at an intersection, turning
left and turning right can both be correct, so scoring a single predicted
trajectory by its $L_2$ distance to the one path the human happened to take says
very little. The other is that open-loop scoring contains no interaction.
Whether to finish an overtake or pull back in depends on what the other agents
do, and only a closed loop shows that.

nuPlan therefore defines three tasks of increasing difficulty. In
\emph{open-loop} the planner imitates the human trajectory and no control
happens. In \emph{non-reactive closed-loop} the planner drives a simulated
vehicle while the other agents replay their recorded tracks. In \emph{reactive
closed-loop} the other agents are driven by a planning model as well. A
controller tracks the planner's output through a kinematic motion model, and the
trajectory that actually gets driven is scored on traffic-rule compliance,
human-likeness, vehicle dynamics and comfort, and whether the goal was reached.

For us, nuPlan supplies the ground-truth future ego trajectories the diffusion
probe is trained on, and enough scenario variety to study multi-modal behaviour,
turns and intersections above all. It also shares an evaluation lineage with
Orbis, whose nuPlan-turns split comes from the same source.

\subsection{The NAVSIM benchmark}
\label{sec:navsim}

nuPlan defines the closed-loop ideal, but running full interactive simulation is
expensive, and for vision-based end-to-end models it is hard to make
photorealistic. NAVSIM~\cite{navsim} works around this with
\emph{pseudo-simulation}. The idea is to keep the scalability and determinism of
open-loop evaluation on real data, while recovering part of what closed-loop
testing actually measures, namely robustness to distribution shift and some
resistance to causal confusion. Before any evaluation runs, a set of synthetic
observations is generated around each real frame with a driving-specific variant
of 3D Gaussian Splatting, so the agent is scored not only on the recorded
observation but also on nearby states it could plausibly end up in.

Evaluation then runs in two stages, sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:navsim}. In
\textbf{Stage~1} the agent predicts a $4$-second trajectory from the real
observation. That trajectory is executed in a simplified bird's-eye-view
simulation with a kinematic bicycle model and an LQR controller, which gives a
score and an endpoint. In \textbf{Stage~2} the same scoring is repeated on the
synthetic observations, whose start points are sampled around the human's true
endpoint: laterally up to $\pm2$\,m, and longitudinally over whatever range is
physically reachable, which yields more samples in faster scenarios. Each
Stage-2 observation comes with a plausible heading and motion history, obtained
by matching against real driving data. The Stage-2 scores are combined with a
Gaussian weighting on how close each synthetic start point is to the Stage-1
predicted endpoint, so that futures the agent is actually likely to reach count
for more:
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{combined}} = s_1\cdot\sum_i \hat{w}^{\,i}\,s_2^{\,i},
\qquad
\hat{w}^{\,i} = \frac{w^i}{\sum_j w^j},
\quad
w^i = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert x^i - \hat{x}\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big),
\end{equation}
Here $\hat{x}$ is the Stage-1 endpoint and $x^i$ the start point of the $i$-th
synthetic scene. The two-stage score correlates noticeably better with true
closed-loop results ($R^2=0.8$) than the best open-loop metric does
($R^2=0.7$), and it needs around $6\times$ fewer environment interactions than
full simulation. The public NAVSIM~v2 leaderboard runs on a hard
\texttt{navhard} split of nuPlan-derived scenes, with unprotected turns and
dense traffic, so that submissions are comparable.

\subsection{PDM and Extended PDM scores}
\label{sec:pdms}

The metric used at each NAVSIM stage is the Predictive Driver Model Score
(PDMS), or its extension EPDMS, in the form written down in
Hydra-MDP++~\cite{hydra}. Both have the same shape: a product of binary
\emph{penalty} sub-scores, any one of which can zero the whole thing if the
violation is serious enough, multiplied by a weighted average of graded
\emph{quality} sub-scores. The original score is
\begin{equation}
\text{PDMS} =
\underbrace{\text{NC}\cdot\text{DAC}}_{\text{penalties}}
\cdot
\underbrace{\frac{5\cdot\text{TTC} + 2\cdot\text{C} + 5\cdot\text{EP}}{12}}_{\text{weighted average}},
\label{eq:pdms}
\end{equation}
where NC is no at-fault collision, DAC is drivable-area compliance, TTC is
time-to-collision, C is comfort, and EP is ego progress, measured against a
rule-based reference planner. Since NC and DAC multiply, a collision or driving
off the road sends the score to zero no matter how smooth or fast the trajectory
was.

EPDMS adds terms for failures the original score did not catch: traffic-light
compliance, lane keeping, driving direction, and a longer-horizon comfort term.
\begin{equation}
\text{EPDMS} =
\underbrace{\text{NC}\cdot\text{DAC}\cdot\text{DDC}\cdot\text{TL}}_{\text{penalties}}
\cdot
\underbrace{\frac{5\cdot\text{TTC} + 2\cdot\text{C} + 5\cdot\text{EP} + 5\cdot\text{LK} + 5\cdot\text{EC}}{22}}_{\text{weighted average}},
\label{eq:epdms}
\end{equation}
DDC is driving-direction compliance, TL is traffic-light compliance, LK is lane
keeping, and EC is extended comfort, which measures how consistent acceleration,
jerk and yaw are across consecutive frames. NAVSIM~v2 also adds a
\emph{filtering} rule: if the human expert committed the same violation in the
same scene, the penalty is waived. This stops the score from punishing maneuvers
that were justified in context, such as briefly crossing into the opposite lane
to get around a static obstacle, on the basis of what is really label noise.

These metrics are what give the trajectory study a vocabulary for evaluation. A
trajectory sampled from the diffusion probe can be scored by more than its
displacement from the human path, which is a metric that correlates poorly with
actual driving quality anyway. We can also ask whether it stays on the drivable
area, avoids collisions, makes progress, and respects driving direction and
comfort. Together with the distribution-level realism and coverage view taken
from Orbis, that lets us ask the things we actually care about for a world-model
probe: whether the predicted trajectories are close to what a human did, whether
they are drivable and safe at all, and whether the spread across samples is
realistic rather than arbitrary.
